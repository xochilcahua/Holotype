Composition of narrative prose about imagined characters and events, in forms ranging from the short story and novella to the novel. Its recognized elements include point of view, characterization, plot, setting, dialogue and theme, and it is divided into genres such as literary, historical, crime, romance, science fiction and fantasy. The modern novel developed in Europe between the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, with Cervantes's Don Quixote often cited as an early landmark. Composition is iterative, moving through drafting, revision and editing, and works reach readers through publishers, magazines and self-publication. Writers who keep at it care more about the third draft than the first, and the habit of finishing something, badly, on a schedule matters more than waiting to feel ready.

Form of literature that uses the aesthetic and rhythmic qualities of language, such as meter, sound patterning, imagery and line division, alongside or in place of ordinary prose meaning. Its traditions include the epic, lyric, ode and sonnet, the Japanese haiku, and, since the nineteenth century, free verse. The earliest named author in writing is usually given as Enheduanna, a Sumerian high priestess of the twenty-third century BCE, though Assyriologists dispute whether she wrote the hymns bearing her name: the only manuscripts were copied some six centuries after her lifetime, in a later dialect of Sumerian than she would have spoken. Poems are typically composed through repeated revision, and circulate in print, in oral performance and online. Poets tend to notice language at the level of the single word, and to want their work read closely by someone who will argue about it.

Writing of scripts for performance in theater, film and television. A script specifies dialogue and action and, in varying detail, staging, and follows a conventional format: the stage play in acts and scenes, the screenplay in scenes headed by sluglines. Dramatic structure, analyzed in Aristotle's Poetics and later in three-act models, governs conflict and pacing. Scripts are collaborative documents, developed through readings, workshops and revisions with directors, producers and script editors. Writers for the stage or screen are, underneath, editors: they are always cutting, and most of their skill is knowing what a scene cannot do without.

Nonfiction writing presents factual material in prose and includes the essay, memoir, biography and journalism. Literary or narrative nonfiction, sometimes called creative nonfiction, applies techniques associated with fiction, such as scene, characterization and structure, to verifiable subjects. Michel de Montaigne's Essais (1580) established the modern essay, and the New Journalism of the 1960s, associated with Tom Wolfe, Gay Talese and Joan Didion, popularized the narrative approach. Professional standards center on research, sourcing, verification and attribution. Anyone who writes nonfiction has to decide what is worth saying out loud, and that selection is the whole discipline: it favors people with strong opinions and a tolerance for verifying things.

A constructed language, or conlang, is a language whose phonology, grammar and vocabulary are consciously devised rather than having evolved naturally. Constructed languages are created for international communication, as with Esperanto, published by L. L. Zamenhof in 1887; for fiction, as with Tolkien's Quenya and Sindarin, Marc Okrand's Klingon and David J. Peterson's Dothraki; or for experiment and personal art. Design draws on descriptive linguistics, especially phonotactics, morphology and syntax, and is documented in grammars and dictionaries, whose compilation is the field of lexicography. People who build languages tend to be the sort who notice a word in a film and cannot let it go until they know where it came from.

Cryptographic art is work in which a message or image is concealed or encoded using techniques from cryptography and steganography. Methods include substitution and transposition ciphers, acrostics, hidden or microscopic text, and digital steganography that embeds data in images or sound. Kryptos, a sculpture by Jim Sanborn installed at the Central Intelligence Agency headquarters in Langley, Virginia, in 1990, carries four encrypted passages, the last of which has long resisted decipherment. The practice overlaps with puzzle design, codebreaking communities and alternate reality games. The pleasure of a cipher is the same pleasure as a locked door: the people who make them are usually the ones who like being the only one who understands.

An event score, or instruction piece, is a brief text prescribing an action or situation to be carried out by the reader or a performer. The form was developed in the late 1950s and 1960s by artists associated with Fluxus and with John Cage's classes at the New School, including George Brecht, La Monte Young, Alison Knowles and Yoko Ono, whose book Grapefruit (1964) collects such instructions. The work exists as the written score and in each realization, so the same score can yield different results, and authorship is shared between the writer and the performer.

Visual art in which marks are made on a surface, most often paper, by applying a dry medium such as graphite, charcoal, chalk or pastel, or a liquid one such as ink. It is used for observation and study, for planning other works, and as an independent art form; techniques include line, hatching and cross-hatching, stippling, tonal shading and gesture drawing. Drawings survive from the Upper Paleolithic, and drawing was central to academic training from the Renaissance, when disegno was regarded as the foundation of painting, sculpture and architecture. People who draw tend to prefer looking for a long time before starting, and are often more comfortable drawing than talking about what they make.

Nearly everybody has done it, usually at school, and that is a real answer even at a 1. Production of durable images by recording light, either chemically on light-sensitive material or electronically with an image sensor. The first permanent photograph is generally attributed to Nicéphore Niépce in the 1820s; Louis Daguerre's daguerreotype (1839) and William Henry Fox Talbot's calotype negative process established the medium commercially. Its fields include portraiture, documentary, photojournalism, landscape and fine-art practice. Digital cameras, dominant from the early 2000s, and later smartphone cameras made photography a near-universal means of recording and communication. Photographers tend to be patient observers who would rather wait hours for the light than adjust the scene, and who notice immediately when something has been staged.

Practice of planning visual communication through typography, imagery, color and layout. Its products include identity systems and logos, posters, books and periodicals, packaging, signage and digital interfaces. The term is attributed to William Addison Dwiggins, who used it in 1922. The discipline was shaped by movements such as the Bauhaus and the International Typographic Style, which emphasized grid systems and sans-serif type, and is now practiced largely with software such as Adobe Illustrator, InDesign and Figma. Designers are trained to make things legible to strangers, which tends to make them tolerant of other people not immediately getting it.

Art of decorative handwriting and lettering executed with a broad-edged or pointed pen, brush or reed. Major traditions include Western scripts such as Roman capitals, Uncial, Carolingian minuscule, Italic and Copperplate; Arabic and Persian styles including Naskh, Thuluth and Nastaliq; and East Asian brush calligraphy, regarded in China since antiquity as one of the highest art forms. Practice depends on stroke order, letter proportion, pen angle and rhythm, and calligraphers commonly work from exemplars by master scribes. Calligraphers tend to care more about the quality of a single stroke than about what the words mean, and to find this satisfying rather than limiting.

Medium in which images, usually in sequence, are combined with text to convey narrative or ideas. Its forms include newspaper strips, comic books, graphic novels, Japanese manga and webcomics. Rodolphe Töpffer's Swiss picture stories of the 1830s are often cited as early antecedents, and the modern superhero comic book emerged in the United States with Action Comics No. 1 in 1938. Production involves scripting, layout, penciling, inking, coloring and lettering, done by a single creator or a team. Process of creating artworks by transferring ink from a matrix, such as a carved block, etched metal plate, lithographic stone or screen, onto paper or another support. The principal techniques are relief (woodcut, linocut), intaglio (engraving, etching, aquatint), planographic (lithography) and stencil (screen printing). Woodblock printing is attested in China by the ninth century, and European printmakers such as Albrecht Dürer and Rembrandt made the medium a major art form. Prints are usually issued in numbered limited editions signed by the artist.

Depiction of plants with the scientific accuracy needed for identification, part of the wider tradition of scientific illustration that also covers animals, anatomy and other specimens. It developed alongside herbals and natural history, reaching a high point in the eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries with Georg Dionysius Ehret and Pierre-Joseph Redouté, and in Maria Sibylla Merian's studies of insect metamorphosis. Illustrators work from living or preserved specimens, often under magnification, recording diagnostic features in watercolor, graphite or pen and ink. The genre remains in use in floras and taxonomic descriptions. Botanical illustrators are the sort who will draw a plant for a month, and who find the slowness is what makes the accuracy possible.

Technique in which pieces of paper, photographs, fabric or other found materials are cut and adhered to a surface to form a composition. Cut-paper work is known in East Asia from about the tenth century, but collage entered Western fine art around 1912 with the papiers collés of Georges Braque and Pablo Picasso. It was extended by Dadaists such as Hannah Höch and Kurt Schwitters through photomontage and Merz work, by Henri Matisse in his late cut-outs, and by Romare Bearden and Robert Rauschenberg. People who collage rarely start with a plan for the finished thing; they know roughly what they want and find the pieces that satisfy it.

Graffiti is writing or drawing made on a wall or other public surface, usually without authorization. Modern graffiti writing developed in Philadelphia and New York City in the late 1960s and 1970s, when writers spread tags across subway systems and formalized styles such as bubble letters and wildstyle. Street art is a broader label covering murals, stencils, stickers and paste-ups, associated with artists such as Banksy and Shepard Fairey and, in his early SAMO tags, Jean-Michel Basquiat. Unsanctioned work is often illegal, though commissioned murals and designated walls are widespread. Street artists work in daylight with strangers walking past, which tends to produce people who are quick, unprecious about failure, and attached to the location itself.

The technique of producing moving images from a sequence of drawings, each differing slightly from the last, photographed or scanned frame by frame, also called traditional animation. The classic studio method uses key drawings, in-betweens and cels, transparent celluloid sheets on which characters are inked and painted over a separate background. Émile Cohl's Fantasmagorie (1908) is an early example, and Walt Disney's Snow White and the Seven Dwarfs (1937) was a landmark full-length cel-animated feature. Twelve to twenty-four drawings per second of film are typical, and digital tools have largely replaced paper and cel workflows. Animation on paper is slow, physical and unforgiving about timing, and the people who do it become meticulous about motion in a way that carries elsewhere.

Branch of the visual arts that makes three-dimensional works by carving, modeling, casting or assembling materials such as stone, wood, clay, metal, plaster and glass. Traditional techniques are subtractive (carving), additive (modeling) and casting, including lost-wax bronze casting. Sculpture ranges from freestanding figures to reliefs and from monuments to installations. Sculptors tend to work in multiples of a single idea, and to be more interested in the material's behavior than in the finished object's meaning.

The art and technology of making objects from clay and other inorganic, nonmetallic materials hardened by firing. Pottery is the ceramic ware made for use and decoration, including earthenware, stoneware and porcelain, which differ in clay body and firing temperature. Forming methods include hand building (pinch, coil, slab), wheel throwing and slip casting, and surfaces are finished with slips and glazes. Fired ceramic vessels date to about 18,000 BCE in East Asia, and porcelain was developed in China during the first millennium CE. Working in clay is mostly learning what the material will not do, and potters are usually the sort of people who are happy to be surprised by their own work.

Craft of making objects, structures and furniture from wood, using measuring, cutting, shaping and joining techniques. Hand tools include saws, planes, chisels and marking gauges; machine tools include table saws, routers and lathes. Traditional joints such as the dovetail, mortise-and-tenon and finger joint allow assembly without metal fasteners. Subfields include carpentry, joinery, cabinetmaking, turning and carving. Woodworking is among the oldest crafts, with Egyptian furniture and tools surviving from the third millennium BCE, and depends on knowledge of grain direction and wood movement. Woodwork is a craft of reading a board before cutting it, and the people who take it up tend to become particular about timber in a way that colors everything else.

Glassforming technique in which molten glass is inflated into a bubble on the end of a blowpipe. It was invented by glassmakers in the Syro-Palestinian region in the first century BCE and spread rapidly across the Roman Empire. Glass is gathered from a furnace at temperatures above 1,000 degrees Celsius, shaped by rolling, blowing, jacking and paddling, and cooled gradually in an annealer to relieve stress. The Venetian island of Murano is a historic center, and the Studio Glass movement began in 1962 with workshops led by Harvey Littleton. Glassblowing happens in a window of a few minutes and cannot be paused, and the people who do it get used to deciding quickly.

Craft of designing and fabricating ornaments for the body, such as rings, necklaces, bracelets and brooches, from metals, gemstones, enamel and other materials. Standard techniques include sawing, filing, soldering, lost-wax casting, stone setting, engraving and polishing. Jewelry is among the oldest human artifacts; shell beads about 75,000 years old have been found at Blombos Cave in South Africa. Contemporary practice ranges from bench jewelers working in precious metals to art jewelry, which treats the wearable object as sculpture. Jewelry is small-scale problem solving in a precious material, and it attracts people who are more interested in the construction than the decoration.

Textile-production method in which two sets of threads, the vertical warp and the horizontal weft, are interlaced to form cloth. The basic weaves are plain, twill and satin, and more complex structures produce damask, brocade and tapestry. Looms range from the backstrap and warp-weighted types to the treadle-operated floor loom. Woven textiles are documented from the Neolithic, and the Jacquard loom of 1804 used punched cards to control patterns, a precursor of computer programming. Weaving is central to the cultural heritage of the Andes, West Africa and the Near East. Weavers are counting, always, and the people who take it up tend to be precise about materials in a way that shows up in the rest of their lives.

Application of design, aesthetics and construction knowledge to clothing and accessories. Designers develop collections through research, sketching, draping and pattern development, select textiles and trims, and produce them as haute couture, ready-to-wear or mass-market lines. The couture system is generally dated to Charles Frederick Worth's Paris house in the 1850s, and the seasonal calendar centers on fashion weeks in Paris, Milan, London and New York. Conceptual fashion treats garments as an art form, exploring form and meaning beyond wearability.

The craft of joining materials with stitches made with a needle and thread, by hand or with a sewing machine, and is the basis of garment construction. Tailoring is a specialized branch in which structured garments, especially suits and coats, are cut from fitted patterns and shaped by canvassing, padding and pressing. Sewing machines became commercially practical in the mid-nineteenth century through the designs of Elias Howe and Isaac Singer. Skills include pattern reading and cutting, seam finishes, darts and fitting, and bespoke tailoring is exemplified by London's Savile Row. Sewing and tailoring is fitting a two-dimensional pattern to a moving body, and the people who do it are usually very good at seeing how someone actually stands.

Sewing machines became commercially practical in the mid-nineteenth century through the designs of Elias Howe and Isaac Singer. Skills include pattern reading and cutting, seam finishes, darts and fitting, and bespoke tailoring is exemplified by London's Savile Row.

Japanese art of folding paper into decorative or representational forms, traditionally without cutting or gluing. The word derives from ori, folding, and kami, paper. Traditional models such as the crane have been joined by modern designs shaped by mathematical study: Akira Yoshizawa developed a notation system in the twentieth century, and Robert Lang applied computational methods, including circle packing, to complex figures. Folded structures also inform engineering applications such as deployable solar arrays and stents. Kirigami permits cutting, and modular origami assembles multiple sheets. Folding paper is a puzzle with a correct answer, which is a different pleasure from making, and origami people tend to enjoy the constraint more than the result.

A painting technique in which pigment is mixed with heated beeswax, often with damar resin, applied to a rigid support and fused with heat. The medium was used in the Fayum mummy portraits of Roman Egypt, dating from the first to third centuries CE, and in ancient Greek decoration of ships and temples. It was revived in the twentieth century, notably by Jasper Johns, whose Flag (1954 to 1955) used encaustic over newspaper. The wax sets quickly and can be layered, scraped, incised or reworked by reheating; ventilation and controlled heating are required. Encaustic painting is done with a hot iron and sets fast, so the painter has to commit in a second, which suits people who prefer deciding to deliberating.

Art of creating images from small pieces of colored glass, stone, ceramic or other material, called tesserae, set in adhesive or mortar. It is documented in Mesopotamia and was developed in Greek and Roman pavements; Byzantine mosaics of the fifth to fifteenth centuries, such as those at Ravenna and in the Hagia Sophia, used gold-glass tesserae to decorate walls and vaults. Methods include the direct, indirect and double-indirect techniques. Modern mosaic includes the trencadís of Antoni Gaudí, made from broken ceramic pieces. Mosaic is the art of making a picture out of what fits the gaps, so the people who do it tend to be more interested in the assembly than in the drawing.

Rogue taxidermy, a term associated with the Minnesota Association of Rogue Taxidermists, founded in 2004, refers to artistic work that departs from naturalistic representation by combining species, inventing hybrid creatures or using animal remains in sculpture and assemblage. The practice draws on Victorian curiosities such as Walter Potter's anthropomorphic tableaux. Practitioners commonly use animals that died of natural causes or by accident, and work is subject to wildlife regulations on species and sale. Taxidermy requires being matter-of-fact about a dead animal, and the people who can do it well are almost always the ones who seem least disturbed by that.

Composers organize pitch, rhythm, harmony, timbre, form and texture, and work in genres from symphony and opera to song and electronic music. Western notation, developed from medieval neumes into the staff system, allowed composition to be separated from performance, and the profession of composer emerged in the Renaissance and Baroque periods. Instrumental performance is the playing of a musical instrument, alone or in ensemble. Instruments are traditionally grouped by how sound is produced, as strings, woodwinds, brass, percussion and keyboards, and scholars use the Hornbostel-Sachs scheme of chordophones, aerophones, membranophones and idiophones, with electrophones added later. Technique involves control of tone, intonation, articulation, rhythm and dynamics, and repertoire spans classical, folk, jazz and popular traditions. Formal conservatory training developed in Europe from the eighteenth century and combines individual lessons, études and ensemble playing. Playing an instrument is largely a question of what you choose to practice, and players tend to have firm views about tone, touch and what counts as good playing.

Vocal types in Western classical music include soprano, mezzo-soprano, alto, tenor, baritone and bass, and styles range from opera, choral music and art song to folk, jazz, pop and traditions such as Mongolian throat singing. Vocal pedagogy developed with the bel canto teaching of eighteenth-century Italy, and singing is practiced both solo and in choirs. Spontaneous creation of music during performance, without full advance composition. It is central to jazz, blues and flamenco and to Indian classical music, where improvisation follows the melodic frameworks of raga, and it was a routine skill of Baroque keyboard players, who realized figured bass and ornamented melodies. Improvisers draw on learned vocabularies of scales, chord progressions, rhythmic patterns and stylistic idioms, and respond to other performers in real time. Free improvisation, developed in the 1960s, dispenses with predetermined harmonic or formal structures. Improvisers are above all listeners, and building something out of whatever has just happened transfers well to everything else they do.

Audio mixing is the process of combining multitrack recordings into a stereo or surround mix, adjusting levels, panning, equalization, dynamics and effects such as reverb. Mastering is the final stage of audio post-production, in which the completed mix is prepared for distribution, ensuring consistent loudness, tonal balance and format compliance. Both depend on critical listening in calibrated monitoring conditions. The practices developed with multitrack recording in the 1950s and 1960s and were transformed by digital audio workstations and by loudness standards such as EBU R128. Mixing and mastering is where the decisions are invisible and the results arguable, which suits people who are happy to have the last word.

Autonomous sensory meridian response, or ASMR, is a tingling sensation, often described as beginning at the scalp, reported by some people in response to specific auditory or visual stimuli such as whispering, tapping, crinkling and personal attention role-play. The term was coined in 2010 by Jennifer Allen. ASMR content is produced largely for video platforms, using close-microphone and binaural recording techniques to create a sense of proximity, and by the mid-2010s had become a large genre on YouTube. Scientific study of the phenomenon remains limited, though several small studies have measured physiological relaxation responses in susceptible viewers.

Directing of a musical ensemble, such as an orchestra, choir or opera company, through visual gesture, coordinating tempo, dynamics, phrasing and entries. The conductor typically uses a baton and follows or interprets a full score. The role became prominent in the nineteenth century as orchestras grew larger and repertoire more complex, with figures such as Hector Berlioz and Richard Wagner writing on the art, and Arturo Toscanini and Herbert von Karajan later exemplifying its interpretive authority. Conductors study score analysis, rehearsal technique and baton patterns such as the standard two, three and four beat. Conductors are the rare people paid to be visibly in charge of a large group without doing the work, and most found that harder than expected.

Acrobatics comprises athletic skills of balance, agility and body control, including tumbling, handstands, balances and aerial rotation, performed individually or with partners. It is a component of gymnastics, circus and cheerleading and has roots in ancient practices documented in Minoan bull-leaping frescoes and Chinese and Egyptian antiquity. Training progresses through conditioning, flexibility work and spotted skill progressions to manage the risks of falls and impact. Circus acrobatics was formalized in eighteenth-century England by Philip Astley, regarded as the founder of the modern circus, and later shaped by companies such as Cirque du Soleil. Acrobats learn to fall before they learn anything else, and the people who stay in it tend to be comfortable with failure.

Magic, as a performing art, is the theatrical presentation of feats that appear to defy natural law, achieved through sleight of hand, misdirection, mechanical devices and psychological technique. Historical development runs from Reginald Scot's The Discoverie of Witchcraft (1584) through the nineteenth-century theatrical magic of Jean Eugène Robert-Houdin, regarded as the father of modern conjuring, to Harry Houdini's escapology and later close-up and stage performers. Magic is generally classed by method, such as card magic, coin magic and mentalism, and technique is transmitted through books, magic societies and mentorship, with secrecy governing many effects. Stage magic depends entirely on an audience believing something that is not happening, and the people who do it well are good readers of a room.

Organizations such as the Society of American Fight Directors, founded in 1977, certify stage combat instructors and techniques. Wrestling promotions including the World Wrestling Federation, renamed WWE in 2002, developed the form into a globally broadcast entertainment industry. Both fields depend on trust, timing and rehearsed contact to protect performers. Stage combat is choreography with real risk attached, and it needs people who can be aggressive on stage and extremely careful with each other off it.

Because the work is temporal, it is documented through photography, video and written score, and is sometimes re-performed by the original or other artists.

The modern director emerged in the late nineteenth century, with figures such as Georg II, Duke of Saxe-Meiningen, and later Konstantin Stanislavski establishing directorial authority over staging and ensemble performance, distinct from the earlier actor-manager model. Work includes casting, blocking, or arranging actors' movement on stage, and running rehearsals through to opening. Early editing theory developed in the Soviet Union through Lev Kuleshov's experiments and Sergei Eisenstein's writing on montage in the 1920s. Editing moved from physical cutting and splicing of celluloid, using tools such as the Moviola and Steenbeck, to nonlinear digital systems from the late 1980s onward, including Avid Media Composer and, later, DaVinci Resolve and Adobe Premiere Pro. Projection mapping is a technique that uses video projectors to display images or animation onto irregular surfaces, such as buildings, objects or terrain, calibrated so the projected content aligns precisely with the physical form. It developed from architectural video projection experiments in the 1960s and became widespread with software such as Resolume and TouchDesigner and the spread of affordable high-lumen projectors in the 2000s and 2010s. Applications include building facade shows, concert and event visuals, museum installations and theatrical set design, and the related practice of light art also uses LEDs, lasers and other controllable light sources. It treats light as the artwork itself, in public or outdoor spaces, which sets it apart from lighting design, where light serves a production or a building. Light and projection work is technical in the worst sense and brief in the best, and the artists who do it care a great deal about calibration.

Competitive sand sculpture is showcased at events such as the World Championship, and large-scale works rely on a mixture of sand and water compacted in forms to hold detail; ice carving is a staple of culinary competitions and winter festivals such as the Harbin International Ice and Snow Sculpture Festival, begun in 1985. Sand and ice sculpture is guaranteed to be destroyed, and the people who make it would rather be spectacular for a week than permanent for a decade.

A happening is a live, often participatory art event that blurs the line between artwork and audience, developed by Allan Kaprow, who coined the term for his 1959 work 18 Happenings in 6 Parts. Relational aesthetics, a term proposed by curator Nicolas Bourriaud in 1998, describes art from the 1990s onward, such as Rirkrit Tiravanija's gallery meals, that takes social interaction and human relations as its primary medium and material. Both traditions treat the structured situation, rather than an object, as the artwork, and depend on documentation, since the events themselves are transient.

A readymade is an ordinary manufactured object designated as an artwork by an artist, with little or no alteration, a category introduced by Marcel Duchamp beginning around 1913 to 1917. His Fountain (1917), a porcelain urinal signed "R. Mutt," submitted to the Society of Independent Artists, is the most cited example and prompted lasting debate over the definition of art. The strategy shifted emphasis from the artist's manual skill to selection, context and the accompanying idea, influencing Conceptual art and later appropriation art. Duchamp distinguished the assisted readymade, which involves minor modification, from the pure readymade.

Erasure, or blackout, poetry is created by selectively obscuring most of the words on an existing page, such as a newspaper article, legal document or page from a book, leaving the remaining visible words to form a new poem. Tom Phillips's A Humument, begun in 1966 and reworked over decades, treats an entire obscure Victorian novel as source material through painted and collaged erasure. Other notable examples include Ronald Johnson's Radi Os (1977), derived from Milton's Paradise Lost, and Jonathan Safran Foer's Tree of Codes (2010), cut from Bruno Schulz's prose. Blackout poetry strikes words out of a found text so the survivors carry the meaning, and the poets who do it like constraints that force an argument.

Textile technique that creates patterns and structure purely through knotting cord, with no weaving, needle or loom involved, most often using the square knot and half hitch as its two foundational moves. The name derives from an Arabic word for a fringed veil or towel, and the technique spread through nineteenth-century European sailors, who knotted rope into decorative work during long sea voyages, giving it a lasting association with maritime and bohemian craft. Cord thickness and material, from fine cotton to thick jute, largely determine the finished texture and scale of a piece, from delicate jewelry to large-scale wall hangings and plant hangers. Because every pattern is built from the same handful of knots, the craft is unusually quick to learn but slow to master at complex, multi-cord scale. Macramé is knotwork with no loom and no pattern book, and the people who do it enjoy inventing the structure as they go.

Constructs patterns, jewelry and small sculptural objects by stringing, stitching or loom-weaving beads, most often small glass seed beads, according to a charted grid pattern where each square represents one bead. Beadwork has deep, independent traditions on multiple continents, including intricate loomed and peyote-stitch work developed by numerous Indigenous nations of the Great Plains and Great Lakes regions of North America, and Maasai beadwork in East Africa, each with its own color vocabulary and symbolism. Off-loom stitches such as peyote and brick stitch build fabric bead-by-bead using only a needle and thread, allowing curved and three-dimensional forms that a loom cannot produce. The craft rewards precise counting and tension, since a single miscounted row shifts every bead after it. Beadwork is thousands of tiny decisions, and beadworkers are usually the people in the room who will notice the one bead that is wrong.

Candle making shapes melted wax, most often soy, paraffin or beeswax, into a candle by pouring it around a wick set in a mold or container, and typically incorporates fragrance oils and dye for scent and color. Candles for light predate written history, with tallow and beeswax candles documented in ancient Egypt and Rome, though the craft today is practiced almost entirely for scent, mood and decorative object-making rather than illumination. Success depends on matching a wax's melting point and additive ratios to its wick size and container, since a mismatch causes tunneling, poor scent throw or excess soot, details usually worked out through small test batches. Because the wax sets and can't be substantially reworked once poured, most of the craft's decisions happen before the pour, not after. Candle making is one of the few crafts where a beginner's first attempt is genuinely good, which makes it an unusually good way to find out whether you enjoy making things at all.

Produces soap through saponification, the chemical reaction between a fat or oil and an alkali, traditionally lye, that converts them into soap and glycerin. The cold-process method, in which the reaction runs to completion inside the mold in a day or two and the soap then cures for several weeks before it is used, gives makers wide control over recipe, scent, color and swirl technique, and has grown as a craft alongside broader interest in handmade, small-batch goods. Historical soap-making dates to ancient Babylon and Rome, though industrial manufacture from the nineteenth century onward made handmade soap a craft revival rather than a necessity. Because lye is caustic, the process requires protective equipment and careful measurement by weight, and once combined the mixture cannot be undone, only worked with, before it fully sets.

Resin art works with liquid epoxy or polyester resin, which cures from a two-part chemical reaction into a hard, often glass-clear, solid, used for pour paintings, jewelry casting, river tables and encapsulating found objects. The medium rewards deliberate loss of control: techniques such as the dirty pour and cell-forming additives rely on the resin's own fluid dynamics and surface tension to generate patterns no brush could produce, and its rise as a hobby medium tracks closely with online video tutorials from the 2010s onward. Working with resin requires good ventilation and protective gloves against its fumes and skin irritation, and, because it cures permanently within a fixed working time, any correction must happen before the pour rather than after. Resin art is unforgiving about timing and bubbles, and the people who stay with it tend to enjoy the small satisfaction of a flawless pour.

Pours molten metal into a prepared mold to form sculpture, most often using the lost-wax process, in which a wax model is encased, melted out, and replaced by molten bronze or another alloy. The technique is one of the oldest in sculpture, documented in bronzes from the Indus Valley civilization and refined through the Renaissance workshops of casters such as Cellini. Because the process requires furnace heat exceeding a thousand degrees Celsius and involves genuine danger from molten metal, it is almost always practiced at a shared foundry under trained supervision rather than solo, and each pour is a single, irreversible event that either succeeds or requires the whole mold to be remade. Surface finishing, called chasing, happens only after the cast has cooled. Metal casting involves melting something and losing the original if it goes wrong, so founders tend to be decisive people with a high tolerance for heat and mess.

Interior design plans the function, layout, materials and finishes of interior spaces, balancing a client's needs and budget against spatial, structural and building-code constraints. Professional practice distinguishes the field from interior decoration, which addresses surface finishes and furnishing rather than spatial planning, load-bearing considerations and code compliance. The discipline professionalized through the twentieth century alongside modern architecture, with figures such as Elsie de Wolfe credited as an early professional decorator and later designers formalizing space planning as its own expertise. Design of a typeface as a complete, consistent system of letterforms, numerals and punctuation intended to work together across countless combinations of words at many sizes. Unlike calligraphy or hand lettering, which produce a single finished piece, a typeface must be drawn as reusable outline data, historically punchcut in metal and today built in specialist software such as Glyphs or FontLab that controls spacing, kerning pairs and, for variable fonts, interpolation between weights. Influential designers include Claude Garamond, whose sixteenth-century types still underlie contemporary revivals, and Zuzana Licko, a pioneer of digital type design at Emigre from the 1980s. A typeface's real test is legibility in ordinary use, not the appeal of any single letter. Type designers spend years on shapes that take a fraction of a second, and they tend to be people who are very particular about the difference between two curves.

Lighting design plans how light shapes mood, focus and legibility within a theater production, concert, architectural space or film set, working through angle, color, intensity and timing rather than pigment or material. In live performance, designers plot a rig of fixtures against a venue's grid, program cues on a lighting console, and refine timing during technical rehearsals so that changes land precisely with the action; contemporary automated fixtures allow color and position to be changed live rather than only by physically re-gelling and re-aiming lamps. AR and VR art builds interactive, spatial experiences using augmented reality overlays on the physical world or fully virtual environments viewed through a headset, where a participant's own movement and viewpoint become part of the work. Practice draws on real-time 3D engines such as Unity and Unreal shared with the video game industry, combined with spatial audio and, increasingly, hand and eye tracking, and typically requires a small interdisciplinary team spanning 3D art, programming and sound design. Documentary filmmaking records real people, places and events and shapes them, through selection, structure and editing, into a nonfiction narrative, working within an inherent tension between observation and authored storytelling. Traditions range from the observational, unobtrusive camera of North American direct cinema, practiced by filmmakers including Frederick Wiseman and D. A. Pennebaker, to the French cinéma vérité of the 1960s, which puts its own framing and editing on screen, to heavily narrated, archival-driven essay films. Because a documentary must adapt to events it cannot script or fully predict, production is far less controlled than fiction filmmaking, and the film's real shape is often found later, in an editing process that can take longer than the shoot itself. Ethical questions around subject consent, representation and the filmmaker's own influence on unfolding events remain central to the form's practice and criticism.

The form flourished as radio drama from the 1920s through the 1950s, with Orson Welles's 1938 broadcast of The War of the Worlds a famous example of its power to convince, before television drew audiences away; it has since revived substantially through serialized fiction podcasts from the 2010s onward. Dry stone walling builds freestanding walls entirely from stacked, uncut fieldstone, held together by careful selection and placement rather than mortar, each stone chosen and fitted to lock against its neighbors and bear weight through its own geometry. UNESCO recognized dry stone walling as intangible cultural heritage in 2018, reflecting concern over the decline of the traditional skill as agricultural walling gave way to fencing. Aquascaping designs and maintains a planted, underwater landscape inside an aquarium, arranging hardscape materials such as driftwood and stone alongside live aquatic plants and, usually, fish, according to compositional principles drawn from landscape painting. The Nature Aquarium style, developed by Japanese aquascaper Takashi Amano beginning in the 1990s, drew explicitly on the asymmetric compositional rules of Japanese landscape art and established aquascaping as an internationally judged competitive art form. Terrarium and vivarium design builds a small, enclosed living landscape inside a glass container, layering drainage material, substrate and live plants, and, in a vivarium, small animals such as frogs or invertebrates, into a self-sustaining miniature ecosystem. Closed terrariums descend from the Wardian case, a closely glazed wooden box with a sealed frame, which Nathaniel Bagshaw Ward developed and published in 1842 after experiments begun in the 1830s, to carry live plants by sea; it incidentally showed that plants could survive for years in a nearly sealed container on their own recycled moisture. Ward took the credit, though a near-identical case had been made about a decade earlier by the botanist A. A. Maconochie, whose failure to publish it left him out of the story. Practice of opening battery-powered electronic toys and simple sound devices and short-circuiting their circuit boards by hand to discover new, usually unintended, sounds, then wiring the most interesting points to switches for repeatable use. The term was coined by musician Reed Ghazala, who described the technique after a toy amplifier with its circuitry exposed shorted against a metal desk drawer in 1967; he began publishing on it, and using the term, in 1992. Because the results depend on undocumented, chip-specific circuit behavior rather than any published design, most discoveries come from direct trial and error with a battery-powered device and a finger, not from schematics, and outcomes are inherently unpredictable. Parkour trains the body to move efficiently over, under and through obstacles in the built environment, using techniques such as vaults, rolls and precision jumps developed to cross terrain as directly and fluidly as possible. The discipline was formalized in French suburbs during the 1980s and 90s by David Belle and the Yamakasi group, drawing on the methode naturelle training philosophy of French naval officer Georges Hebert, and later branched into freerunning, a related but more expressive, acrobatics-influenced variant popularized by Sebastien Foucan. Training happens directly on real urban terrain, walls, railings and rooftops, meaning route-finding is improvised in the moment even though the underlying vocabulary of moves is drilled and conditioned in advance. Because a missed landing carries real injury risk, the discipline places heavy emphasis on progressive, well-spotted practice and honest self-assessment of a jump's difficulty before attempting it. Parkour is about the route rather than the trick, and the people who do it are planners who have already worked out how they will get down.

Prose composed for a named reader rather than a general audience, historically governed by conventions of address, closing and etiquette codified in medieval ars dictaminis manuals and Renaissance letter-writing guides. In true (buon) fresco, pigment ground in water is applied directly to a thin layer of wet lime plaster; as the plaster carbonates over the following hours it locks the color permanently into the wall itself, which is why Roman, Byzantine and Renaissance frescoes have survived intact for centuries. That chemistry imposes a working method unlike any other kind of painting: the artist plans the whole composition in advance as a full-scale cartoon, then plasters and paints only as much wall (a giornata, or day's work) as can be completed before the surface sets, with no way to blend, glaze or correct once an area dries. Michelangelo's Sistine Chapel ceiling and Diego Rivera's twentieth-century murals both worked this way. The irrevocability, physical scale, and reliance on assistants to prepare plaster on schedule make it a fundamentally different discipline from easel painting in oil or acrylic, even though the visual result is also pigment on a surface.

The airbrush, patented in refined form by Charles Burdick in 1893 and popularized through commercial illustration studios in the early twentieth century, replaces the brush's touch with a stream of atomized paint controlled by trigger pressure and stencils or masking film, producing smooth gradients and photorealistic surfaces that hand-brushing cannot easily match. It became the working tool of mid-century advertising illustration and pinup art, then of custom vehicle and motorcycle tank painting, and later of nail art and body art. Arranging and orchestration take a melody or a finished piece and decide how it will actually sound: which instruments or voices carry which lines, how harmony is voiced across a section, what is added, cut or reharmonized, and how the parts are balanced against each other, all written out as a full score and parts rather than performed live. It is distinct from Music Composition, which invents the underlying musical material, and from Conducting, which realizes the arrangement in performance; an arranger's raw material is usually someone else's melody or chord chart. Professional film and Broadway orchestrators such as Robert Russell Bennett, and pop arrangers who built the layered string and horn charts behind Motown and big-band recordings, built entire careers purely on this transformation, working from a skeleton to a fully scored ensemble sound.

Balloon twisting uses the specific give and squeak of long, narrow modeling balloons, twisted, folded and locked into place with friction alone, to build figures (animals, hats, swords) in real time in front of an audience, usually as part of a children's entertainer's or street performer's act. Modern latex modeling balloons date to the mid-twentieth century, and the craft was systematized and spread internationally through entertainer conventions and instructional videos from the 1980s onward, developing a shared vocabulary of standard twists (bubble, pinch, fold) that combine into more complex builds the way basic knots combine in Macrame. Because a mistimed twist pops the balloon outright, with no way to undo or repair it, the discipline rewards fast, confident, well-rehearsed hand sequences over slow deliberation, and most performers build an act around a repertoire of twenty or so reliable figures rather than improvising complex new ones live.

Chocolate making begins with tempering: heating and cooling melted chocolate through precise temperature bands so its cocoa butter crystallizes into a stable form that gives a clean snap and shine instead of a dull, soft-melting bloom, a controlled thermal process closer to metallurgy than most cooking. Charcuterie, named for the French chair cuite ('cooked flesh'), takes the trimmings and tougher cuts and preserves them through salting, smoking, air-drying and controlled fermentation, techniques that predate refrigeration by millennia and produce foods, prosciutto, salami, pâté, that depend on precise humidity, temperature and time rather than heat alone. Distilling takes a fermented base, wine, mash or beer, and separates alcohol from water by heating it just past ethanol's boiling point and below water's, then condensing the vapor back into liquid; the craft lies in deciding where to cut the run, discarding the toxic 'heads' and flavorless 'tails' and keeping only the 'heart' of the distillate, along with choices of still design (pot versus column) and barrel aging that shape the final spirit's character. The technique reached Europe through Islamic-world alchemists (the word 'alcohol' itself derives from Arabic al-kuhl) and medieval monastic distillers before spreading into whisky, brandy, rum and gin production. Unlike Brewing, Wine & Spirits fermentation, which is primarily biological and forgiving of imprecision, distilling is a physical separation process governed by exact temperatures and cut points, and in most countries is legally restricted to licensed producers even at hobbyist scale, which sets its access and institutional context apart from home brewing or winemaking. Distilling waits a long time for a small, strong, dangerous result, and the people who do it are usually very comfortable with responsibility.

Sushi centers on two disciplines practiced to a fine tolerance: seasoning and handling rice (shari) so each grain stays separate but coheres when pressed, and cutting fish along its grain with single, clean knife strokes rather than a sawing motion, since a ragged cut changes both texture and how the fish takes seasoning. Traditional training under a sushi master (itamae) in Japan can run a decade or more before an apprentice is trusted to make rice, let alone touch fish, an apprenticeship model far longer than most Western culinary training. Nigiri, hand-pressed rice topped with a single slice of fish, reduces the dish to almost nothing extraneous, which is precisely what makes the smallest errors in rice temperature, knife angle or fish freshness immediately visible; the discipline's minimalism demands more precision, not less, than a dish with more ingredients to hide behind. Sushi is a craft where the knife work is the reputation, and the chefs who last are the ones willing to serve the same thing for a decade.

Thatching builds a waterproof roof entirely from bundled plant stems, traditionally water reed, long straw or combed wheat reed, fixed to the roof structure in overlapping courses so rain always runs off the outer surface of a lower layer rather than penetrating, with no membrane, nail pattern or manufactured waterproofing doing the actual work. It is one of the oldest roofing techniques worldwide and survived in places like the United Kingdom and parts of Northern Europe as a living trade rather than a museum craft, passed down through lengthy on-site apprenticeship because a thatcher has to judge by eye and hand how tightly to dress and fix each bundle. Wood turning spins a block of wood on a lathe and shapes it with hand-held gouges and chisels braced against a tool rest, cutting a symmetrical profile (bowls, spindles, legs, pens) by removing material while it rotates rather than by carving or joining stationary pieces the way general Woodworking does. It splits into two distinct working methods: spindle turning, where the wood grain runs parallel to the lathe's axis, used for chair legs and balusters, and faceplate or bowl turning, where the grain runs across it, used for bowls and platters, each demanding a different tool approach to avoid tear-out. Because the workpiece is moving at speed rather than the tool, turning rewards a steady, controlled cut read by ear and vibration as much as by sight, and catches, where a tool bites unexpectedly, can happen in a fraction of a second, making it a far more continuous, reactive motion than the stop-and-check pace of hand-tool woodworking or carving. Turning wood is one of the few crafts where the material and the tool move together continuously, and turners tend to become absorbed very quickly.

Marquetry builds an image or pattern by cutting thin sheets of colored and grained wood veneer with a fine fretsaw or scroll saw and fitting the pieces together edge to edge like a wood mosaic, then gluing the whole assembled picture onto a solid backing, a technique closer in logic to Mosaic or stained glass than to any other woodworking discipline. Spinning draws out loose fiber, wool, cotton, flax, and adds twist to lock it into a continuous, strong strand, either with a hand-held drop spindle, one of the oldest tools still in everyday use, or a spinning wheel, which mechanized the process from roughly the eleventh century onward and became a central symbol of pre-industrial domestic labor. It is the upstream craft that Weaving and Knitting & Crochet both depend on but rarely include: those disciplines take finished yarn as a raw material, while spinning is entirely about the fiber itself, choosing a fiber's staple length and crimp, drafting it evenly by hand, and controlling twist rate to determine the finished yarn's strength, elasticity and texture. Industrial spinning mills, starting with Richard Arkwright's water frame in 1769, made hand spinning commercially obsolete, but it has persisted and grown as a craft in its own right, particularly among fiber artists who value control over yarn characteristics that commercial mill yarn doesn't offer. Spinning is adding twist until fiber holds itself together, which is a quiet, repetitive pleasure that suits people who like to be doing something with their hands for a long time.

Felting bypasses spinning and weaving entirely, matting loose wool fibers directly into solid fabric or three-dimensional shapes by exploiting the way wool's microscopic scales lock together irreversibly under friction and moisture (wet felting) or repeated barbed-needle stabbing (needle felting). It is likely one of the oldest textile techniques, predating both spinning and weaving in the archaeological record, with felt hats, tents and rugs central to nomadic Central Asian cultures for millennia. Wet felting works flat sheets by rolling soapy, wetted wool under pressure until the fibers permanently tangle, useful for fabric, hats and bags, while needle felting builds sculptural three-dimensional forms by repeatedly stabbing a barbed needle into a mass of wool to compact and shape it locally, closer to a subtractive sculpting motion than to any other fiber technique. Unlike every other textile discipline, there is no thread, no loom and no stitch involved anywhere in the process. Felting is the only textile technique with no thread in it at all, which surprises people who assume all cloth comes from winding something around something.

Lace making builds an openwork fabric out of thread manipulated directly, without any loom, using one of several distinct methods: bobbin lace, where dozens of weighted bobbins are crossed and twisted over a pricked pattern pinned to a pillow; needle lace, built stitch by stitch entirely with a needle and thread, closer to Embroidery in tool but not in structure; and tatting, which knots thread into small rings and chains using a shuttle. Venetian and Flemish lace centers dominated European production from the sixteenth century, when lace collars and cuffs became a major luxury status good, expensive enough that sumptuary laws in several countries restricted who was allowed to wear it. Because the fabric is the pattern of holes as much as the thread itself, a lace maker works from a formal pricked or charted design and has to track dozens of simultaneous bobbin pairs or stitches at once, a level of concurrent pattern-tracking that sets it apart from the single-thread-path logic of knitting or crochet. Lace making is building a structure out of knots with no fabric underneath, and lacemakers tend to be quietly exacting.

Shoemaking builds footwear around a last, a solid foot-shaped form, cutting and lasting leather uppers over it under tension, then hand-stitching or Goodyear-welting the upper to a sole so the shoe holds its shape and can later be resoled rather than discarded. Historically split into two guild traditions, cordwainers who made new shoes from fresh leather and cobblers who repaired old ones, a distinction that has mostly collapsed today but still shows up in the different skills involved: bespoke shoemaking is built around a customer-specific last and can take dozens of hours per pair, while cobbling repair work is fast, high-volume trade skill centered on resoling, patching and reheeling. Because a shoe has to flex with a walking foot while holding a precise shape under repeated stress, the leather has to be worked wet and dried under tension over the last, giving shoemaking a fit-driven, load-bearing engineering problem that most other leatherwork, like Leatherworking's bags and belts, doesn't have to solve. Shoemaking is fitting a last to a foot and getting the break exactly right, and the people who do it are usually the sort who notice other people's shoes.

The technique reached a formal peak in eighteenth-century France, where craftsmen produced lacquered furniture, trays and even architectural ornament from papier-mâché as a cheaper substitute for carved wood, and it remains central to festival culture worldwide, from Mexican piñatas to the giant Carnival float figures built in Viareggio, Italy. Pyrography draws with heat instead of pigment: a temperature-controlled metal tip burns a mark directly into wood or leather, and depth of tone comes from dwell time and tip pressure rather than from ink concentration, so a single continuous stroke can shade from pale tan to near-black just by moving faster or slower across the surface. Mends broken pottery with urushi lacquer, then dusts the still-tacky final layer with gold, silver or platinum powder so the repair is deliberately visible as a shining seam rather than disguised, inverting the usual restoration goal of making damage disappear. Flash mob choreography plans a group performance, usually dance, that participants rehearse separately and in secret, then execute together in an ordinary public space (a train station, a mall) in front of an unsuspecting crowd before dispersing as suddenly as they gathered. The form takes its name from a 2003 New York stunt organized by Bill Wasnick, initially non-artistic and closer to a mass prank, but it was quickly adopted by dancers and marketers alike for its built-in surprise-and-reveal structure, distinct from either a staged theatrical performance with a paying, expectant audience or the unauthorized solo intervention of Guerrilla Art. The organizing challenge is as much logistical as choreographic: coordinating dozens or hundreds of participants who may never fully rehearse together before the live moment, timing a covert signal to start, and managing how quickly the group can convincingly return to normal foot traffic afterward, all while keeping the plan secret from the public it's aimed at.

Training is through schools and lineages, and a play is often built on very small, exact gestures.

Family of Chinese theater forms that combine song, speech, mime, martial arts and acrobatics, including Peking opera (jingju), Kunqu and Cantonese opera. Peking opera took shape in Beijing in the late eighteenth century, and training traditionally begins in childhood. Classical dance-drama from Kerala in south India, in which traditionally male performers in elaborate painted faces, towering headdresses and costumes enact stories from the Mahabharata and Ramayana through mudras, facial expression and footwork, without speaking. Singers and drummers on the chenda and maddalam accompany the action. Performances were traditionally overnight temple events, and training is rigorous, begins young and includes exercises for the eyes and face. Japanese avant-garde dance form that began around 1959 with Tatsumi Hijikata and Kazuo Ohno, often called ankoku butoh (dance of darkness). Dancers, frequently in white body paint, use slow, distorted, sometimes grotesque movement and images from dreams, death or nature to explore the body's states rather than conventional technique. It arose partly in response to postwar Japan and has since spread worldwide with many distinct approaches.
